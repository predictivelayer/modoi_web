Task-based time tracking and planning, the Modoi approach

Aug 23, 2026 Modoi 6 min read

Most time tracking tools are built around one assumption: that you will remember to start and stop a timer. This assumption is wrong for the majority of knowledge workers in the majority of work situations. You get pulled into a call, you forget to stop the timer. You start working before you open the app. You batch your tracking at the end of the day from memory, which is a study in motivated reconstruction rather than accuracy.

The result is time data you cannot trust, which defeats the point of collecting it.

Modoi's time tracking is task-based and automatic. You declare a task. You work on it. Modoi records the time. You do not touch a timer.

What task-based time tracking means

Task-based time tracking means time is recorded against specific tasks in your system, not against generic projects or free-form labels you type at the end of the day.

The distinction matters because it determines what you can do with the data afterward. Generic project tracking tells you that you spent four hours on "Client Work" this week. Task-based tracking tells you that you spent 40 minutes on the proposal draft, 90 minutes on the kick-off call, and 50 minutes on revision, and nothing on the competitive analysis you were supposed to finish.

That second picture is actually useful. The first one is noise with a timestamp.

Modoi attaches time to tasks automatically by observing which task is active and what applications you have open. You declare the task you are working on — from a Linear import or one you made locally — and Modoi watches every window for work on it, using the issue title, branch name, and your own keywords. When your windows stop matching, the time is cut there. Entries arrive pending, and you can approve, reject, rename, or retarget them afterward, but in most cases you do not need to.

Why automatic tracking beats manual timers

The obvious argument for automatic tracking is convenience. But convenience is not the real reason it produces better data.

Manual timers have a selection bias problem. People start timers when they remember to, which tends to be when they feel good about what they are about to do. They forget to start them when they are doing lower-status work, doing something they did not plan to do, or doing something they are mildly ashamed to be spending time on. The resulting data reflects motivated self-reporting, not actual behavior.

Automatic tracking has no selection bias. It records everything you do, including the things you would rather not see.

After a week of automatic task-based tracking in Modoi, most people find at least one pattern they were not aware of. Usually it is either that a specific type of task takes significantly longer than they estimated, or that a specific type of task is consuming time they did not realize they were giving it. Both insights are useful. Neither would surface from a timer you start voluntarily.

Planning from time data

The planning side of task-based time tracking is where the data starts paying back.

When Modoi has two or three weeks of accurate task time data, you have something most productivity systems never give you: a calibrated sense of how long things actually take you. Not how long they should take. Not how long similar tasks took someone else. How long they take you.

This makes planning meaningfully different. When you sit down to plan your week and see that "write blog post" has historically taken you 2.5 hours in a single sitting, you stop scheduling it into 45-minute gaps between meetings. When you see that design reviews consistently run over their estimated time, you stop back-to-back scheduling them.

Estimation based on your own history is more reliable than estimation based on optimism, and it does not require any extra work to produce because Modoi builds it from tracking data you collected while doing other things.

How this compares to other approaches

Toggl and Harvest are the standard references for time tracking in productivity contexts. They are good tools. Both require manual timer management, and both organize time around projects rather than tasks by default. They are designed primarily for billing, which is a legitimate use case and a different one from personal productivity.

TickTick has Pomodoro-based time tracking, which is interval-based rather than task-based. The distinction is that a Pomodoro timer counts down from 25 minutes regardless of whether you are working on one task or three. Task-based tracking in Modoi is tied to specific task activity.

For teams that need to bill clients by the hour, Toggl or Harvest are more appropriate. For professionals who want to understand how they actually spend their time and plan better as a result, task-based automatic tracking is more useful.
