Visual day view of work by task: see your whole day in Modoi

Aug 23, 2026 Modoi 6 min read

The most common approach to planning a workday is time blocking on a calendar. You take your tasks, estimate how long each one will take, drag them into calendar slots, and tell yourself you will follow the plan. Most people who do this for more than a week develop a complicated relationship with their 9am blocks that never happened.

The problem with time blocking is that it treats task execution as predictable. Your calendar block for "write Q3 roadmap doc" assumes you know when you will start, how long it will take, and that nothing will interrupt it. None of those assumptions hold reliably for knowledge workers.

Modoi's visual day view takes a different approach. Rather than asking you to predict your day, it shows you your day as it actually happened, organized by task.

What the visual day view shows

Modoi builds a visual timeline of your workday from the time it collects automatically as you work. Each stretch of work gets a block sized to the time it spans. The blocks run chronologically down a light rail across columns for activity, attributed events, projects, calendar, and labels — so you can read the day by what you actually did, by what you tagged it to, or against the meetings that were on the calendar. Colors keep the columns distinct at a glance.

What you see is an honest record of the day. Not the plan. The record.

If you spent two hours on email when you meant to spend 20 minutes, that shows up. If you worked on five different tasks in a fragmented morning, that shows up too. The visual format makes patterns legible in a way that a list of time entries does not.

Most people have very poor intuitions about how they actually spend their time. After a week of looking at the day view, you know things about your work patterns that you did not know before, and some of them are uncomfortable.

How it differs from a task list

A task list tells you what you need to do. A visual day view tells you what you did and when. These are related but different problems.

The task list is forward-looking. The visual day view is retrospective, and it updates in real time as you work. They are most useful together: you plan with the task list and review with the day view.

The distinction from calendar time blocking is sharper. A time-blocked calendar is also forward-looking, and it requires manual entry, estimation, and constant re-planning when the day deviates from the plan. Modoi's day view is built automatically from actual work, so it requires nothing from you beyond declaring your tasks. The difference in ongoing maintenance is significant.

Why organizing by task matters more than organizing by time

Calendar-first day views organize your time into slots, then ask you to fit tasks into those slots. The fundamental unit is the hour.

Task-first day views organize your time around what you were trying to accomplish. The fundamental unit is the task.

This gives you different information. You can see not just that you worked from 9 to 11, but that those two hours were split across four tasks with no single task getting more than 30 minutes of sustained attention. A calendar cannot show you that. A task-organized day view does, without any manual setup.

For anyone trying to understand why certain work is getting done and other work is not, the task-organized view surfaces the answer faster than any other format.

What you can do with the information

The most common realization people have after using a visual task-based day view for a few weeks: the gap between how they intended to spend their time and how they actually spent it is larger than they thought.

A few patterns it regularly surfaces:

  • First-hour fragmentation. Many people spend the first hour of the day context-switching between tasks that feel urgent but are not their most important work. The visual day view makes this visible in a way that reviewing a to-do list never does.
  • Meeting overflow. Time that was supposed to be open for focused work gets consumed by prep and recovery around meetings. Calendar tools undercount this. The task-based day view captures it because it reflects what you actually had active, not what your calendar said you should be doing.
  • Deep work displacement. Tasks requiring sustained attention get pushed to afternoon slots that fragment before they can be used. Once you see this pattern on the day view for three consecutive days, the impulse to protect morning time becomes much less abstract.
